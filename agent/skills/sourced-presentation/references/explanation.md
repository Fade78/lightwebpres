# Editorial explanation and impact

Use when planning the editorial pair or revising wording, interest,
progression or conclusions. These checks concern understanding and impact,
not typography or a target character count.

## Two functions, one evidential chain

- Develop the article as an independently readable argument, not a script
  dictated by the slides. Establish claims and relationships, explain the
  reasoning, examine pertinent objections and retain useful evidence beyond
  the selected cards. A catalogue of citations is not an argument.
- Edit the cards for public attention: choose a point, an angle and the
  supported contrast, consequence or mechanism that makes it matter.
  A faithful summary can still be editorially weak. Improve selection and
  explanation, not the severity or certainty of the facts.
- Give a card enough evidence to earn its takeaway, without reproducing
  the long's entire apparatus. It may reorganize the material for comprehension;
  its claims, implied relationships and necessary qualifications must still
  be supported by the article. Neither end certifies the other.

## Intentions are not audiences or fixed genres

- **Explain or compare:** answer the reader's question using relevant
  criteria and explicit relationships. Do not merely place two descriptions
  beside each other. Impartial examination does not require equal evidential
  weight or a conclusion midway between positions.
- **Review knowledge:** establish what the relevant evidence supports, how
  confidently and where it remains incomplete or inconsistent. A strong
  conclusion is allowed. Disclose the search's actual scope; a narrative
  overview is not automatically a systematic review.
- **Argue a position:** make the thesis, reasoning and any value premises
  visible. Address objections that could change the conclusion; do not
  caricature alternatives. Persuasion may select an angle and emphasis,
  not invent evidence or conceal decisive limitations.
- Combine intentions when useful, but mark the transition from findings
  to interpretation or recommendation. Choose audience knowledge separately.
  An exposition or belief reversal can serve any intention; a scientist
  can defend a thesis and an advocate can compare rigorously.

This is an operational grouping, not a universal taxonomy. University guides
use overlapping categories: [Purdue](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/the_writing_process/thesis_statement_tips.html)
distinguishes explanatory, analytical and argumentative texts;
[Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/students/study-skills/writing/types-of-academic-writing.html)
describes descriptive, analytical, persuasive and critical writing and their
combination. [Harvard](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/counterargument)
explains why an argument must address reasonable alternative answers.

## Plan the point

- Establish subject knowledge and purpose, not just an audience label.
  Scientific readers may be outside the field; their stake may be theoretical.
  Use shared terminology where it helps, otherwise explain it.
- For specialists, expose the relevant design, comparison, measures,
  estimates and uncertainty sufficiently to assess the inference from the
  cards. Identify missing information. The long adds depth, not the sole
  basis for judging the short form.
- For non-specialists, make the question, scene, quantities and comparison
  understandable without increasing certainty. Neither audience needs a
  compulsory anecdote, dramatic hook or ornamental methods preamble.
- Prepare the takeaway from the established article, then the stake,
  plausible objection and explanation. If the evidence changes, change
  the takeaway. A reasonable objection can expose weak evidence, another
  context or a genuine tradeoff, not merely awkward wording.
- Ask why this reader would care. A familiar observation can be clear
  yet uninformative. Find the documented consequence or possibility rather
  than altering evidence to create surprise. Enjoyment and benefit need
  not be denied for a cost to matter.

## Explanation test

Read once in order, stopping before the emphasized conclusion. At each
sentence, answer these questions from what has already been introduced:

| Check | Failure to repair |
|---|---|
| Who acts, on what, and with what result? | Actor, object or operation must be inferred from the writer's private knowledge. |
| What does each pronoun or pointer denote? | “Its replies”, “these uses” or “this result” has competing antecedents. |
| What relation joins the sentences? | Cause, contrast, example, chronology or qualification is merely implied or unsupported. |
| Are the compared objects comparable? | A use is contrasted with a conversation, a duration with a frequency, or a permission with a deletion. |
| Does attribution describe the right actor? | Documentation is credited with performing a product action, or a possibility described in a policy becomes observed practice. |
| Is each needed term or frame already available? | A brand, acronym, task or setting appears before the reader knows its role. |
| Does this detail explain or materially qualify the point? | A subscription aside, secondary procedure or generic warning interrupts the needed chain. |

Rewrite the relationship, not just the connective. Separate operations
that affect different things; make their effects explicit. For example,
changing what appears on a screen and changing what a service retains
require separate explanations. Do not infer either from the other's label.

A conclusion can reveal a new deduction earned by the explanation. It
must not be the first sentence that makes earlier actors, events or
comparisons decipherable. This test does not require stating the final
answer twice or preventing suspense in a properly established account.

## Compose the card

- Give one coherent point its evidence. Keep a scene with its comparison
  when readable; do not split them to assign one card per study. Several
  findings may support one point only when their relationship is established.
- State a supported point in the title. Address relevance in the stake;
  use the body for actions, evidence and results, not advice to the reader
  or self-praise. Introduce the frame before narrating the actions.
- Use concrete actors and verbs. Check clause attachment as well as grammar.
  Do not invent dialogue, motives, sensory detail or characters. A clearly
  hypothetical illustration may explain a mechanism, never serve as observed evidence.
- Remove detours before essential detail. Several paragraphs may be needed
  for one point. Do not trade the scene, comparator or material qualification
  for a short field. Keep ordinary words when technical labels add no precision.
- Do not tell readers what to feel or certify the article's rigor. Avoid
  discussing the article inside the explanation unless sources or method
  are the actual subject. Institutional prestige is not the missing proof.
- Close with the complete supported takeaway, normally one final emphasis.
  Retain the population, comparison, magnitude, time and uncertainty needed
  alone. Keep material favorable and unfavorable outcomes together, without
  inventing balance. An isolated large number must not compete with that point.
- Repeat necessary scope across independent reading paths. A shorter title
  or isolated conclusion must not become more causal, certain or general.
  A generic statement cannot replace the finding's meaningful quantity.
- Give each content card an identifiable source route supporting its point.
  A venue name or series link alone is insufficient. Covers, navigation and
  orientation have different functions; do not fabricate findings for them.

## Connect units without making them dependent

- For each series, page and card, identify entry, gain and next opening.
  Placement is deliberate, but readers can arrive out of order. Supply
  the local premise and context needed to understand each shared unit.
- Let the argument determine progression; do not impose the same sequence
  of problem, danger and solution on every topic. A transition explains
  why the next question follows, not merely where to click next.
- Name links by subject and purpose. Another page can supply depth, not
  a missing referent or the qualification that makes this card true.
- Preserve useful contributions when moving or removing material. Keeping
  a reference or brand name is not enough if its explanation disappears.
  The article can retain findings not selected for the cards.
- Add a source card only when required or useful for how the subject is
  known, usually after the opening. It cannot carry away the material limits
  of all preceding claims.
- End with the established implication. A documented right, setting or
  collective lever can reopen possibilities; do not force an optimistic
  ending or let generic reassurance replace a result.
- When readers may face current harm, provide a relevant, reachable remedy
  under the local safety rules. Verify what it handles. Avoid operational
  details that enable abuse; explanation is not a reproduction guide.
